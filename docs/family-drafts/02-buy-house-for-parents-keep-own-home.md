# Can I buy a house for my parents and keep my own home?

**Often, yes.** Owning a home of your own does not disqualify you. Conventional occupancy
rules include narrow family situations in which a home your parent occupies can be treated as
a primary residence for mortgage purposes even though you live elsewhere and keep your
existing house.

The qualified part of that answer matters. You would generally need to carry both housing
payments in the lender's review, the specific conditions depend on which agency's rules the
loan follows, and individual lenders decide whether they offer the structure at all. If you
want the background on what these provisions are, start with
[what a Family Opportunity Mortgage actually is](/blog/what-is-a-family-opportunity-mortgage).
This article assumes you have that and deals with the homeowner's version of the question.

## Why owning a home does not automatically rule it out

Occupancy classification describes a specific transaction, not your whole life. The question a
lender asks is who will live in the property being financed. In these family provisions, your
parent's occupancy can satisfy that expectation.

Nothing in the relevant Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac occupancy guidance says the borrower must
not already own a home, and neither imposes an age, retirement, or distance requirement. The
constraint is not ownership. It is affordability.

## How your parents intend to occupy the property matters

This has to be real. The provisions exist for a parent who will actually live in the home as
their residence. A property your parents will use occasionally, or that will sit empty, or
that someone else will rent, is a different classification with different rules.

Be equally clear about the opposite case. If your parent will live there and also pay you
something toward the cost, that does not change the occupancy analysis, but it does not help
the way most families assume either. More on that below.

## The conditions that apply

Fannie Mae's provision for a child providing housing for a parent applies where the parent is
unable to work **or** does not have sufficient income to qualify for a mortgage on their own.
Either alone is enough. A parent who works part time but cannot support a mortgage on that
income can still fall within it.

Freddie Mac addresses the same situation in
[Guide Section 4201.11](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/4201.11) and, notably,
states no ability-to-work or income condition for a home occupied by the borrower's parent.

Which set of rules applies depends on the loan, so the honest answer to "does my parent
qualify under the rule" is that it depends on which rule your lender is using. You can read
Fannie Mae's version in its
[occupancy types guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.1-01/occupancy-types).

Separately from the agency rules, individual lenders add their own requirements, including
documentation that the guidance itself does not specify. That is why two lenders can give you
different answers about the same parent.

## Your existing housing costs are part of the review

Here is the practical center of the question. Standard underwriting still applies. Your
current mortgage payment, property taxes, insurance, and any association dues count as an
obligation, and the proposed payment on the new home counts alongside them. So do car loans,
student loans, credit cards, and any other debts.

A lender looks at the whole picture: income, credit, assets, and the total of your
obligations. There is no single debt ratio ceiling that applies across this category, and any
figure quoted to you without a review of your file is guesswork.

What this means in practice is simple. The question is rarely "am I allowed to do this." It is
"do both payments fit."

## Why your parents contributing money does not automatically solve it

Families often assume that a parent paying part of the cost will make the numbers work. It
usually does not, for a reason that surprises people.

Because the property is being treated as a primary residence, rent from it generally cannot be
used as qualifying income. Freddie Mac states directly that rental income generated from a
mortgage secured by a primary residence is not permitted to qualify the borrower, subject to
narrow exceptions, and Fannie Mae takes the same position in its
[rental income guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-01/general-rental-income-information).

You cannot claim the occupancy benefit and also count your parent's payments as income. The
arrangement asks you to qualify as though you were carrying the home yourself.

Your parents' contribution can still matter in other ways. Funds toward the purchase may be
possible depending on the transaction and the lender's rules. That is a different mechanism
from qualifying income, and it is worth asking about specifically rather than assuming.

## What if your parents already own a home?

This comes up constantly and it is worth raising early rather than late.

If your parents own a home they intend to sell, the timing of that sale affects both their
situation and yours. A home that has not sold may still carry a mortgage, and depending on the
structure and whose name is on what, it can affect the review.

If your parents intend to keep their current home and live in the new one, say so plainly at
the start. That changes the picture, and a lender will want to understand it.

Neither situation is automatically disqualifying. Both are the kind of fact that should shape
the plan rather than emerge at underwriting.

## Buying it yourself is not the same as helping them qualify

Two genuinely different routes, often confused:

- **You borrow.** You are the borrower, the debt is yours, your parents occupy. The occupancy
  provisions are what make primary residence treatment possible.
- **Your parents borrow with help.** They are the borrowers and someone else supports the
  application. That involves co-borrower and co-signer roles, which are not interchangeable
  and carry different consequences.

Which fits depends on your parents' credit and income, your own position, and what the family
wants to be true about ownership in ten years.
[Comparing the structures](/blog/family-opportunity-mortgage-vs-cosigning) walks through the
options side by side.

## Ownership, maintenance, and what happens later

Whose name goes on title is a legal question, not a mortgage detail, and it deserves an
attorney rather than a rule of thumb. Neither agency's occupancy guidance requires the
occupying parent to be excluded from title. Fannie Mae's guidance states that only one
borrower must occupy and take title to the property. If a lender tells you your parent must be
off title, that is that lender's requirement, and it is worth knowing it is not an agency rule.

The practical questions are just as important and easier to postpone than they should be:

- Who pays for repairs, and who decides when something gets fixed?
- What happens if your parents' needs change and the home no longer suits them?
- What happens to the property if you need to sell, or if your own circumstances change?
- How does this fit with the rest of your family's planning?

Occupancy classification does not answer any of these. It also does not determine tax
deductions, homestead exemptions, or how the property is treated in an estate. Those belong
with a tax adviser and an attorney.

## Before the first call

Have these available to make the conversation useful. You do not need to upload anything, and
this site is not the place to send documents.

- Roughly what you owe and pay each month on your current home, including taxes and insurance.
- Your other monthly obligations.
- A general sense of your income and how it is documented.
- The price range and area you are considering.
- Whether your parents currently own or rent, and whether a sale is involved.
- Your parents' situation in general terms, meaning whether they work and whether their income
  could support a mortgage on its own.
- Who the family expects to be on title.

That is enough for a first conversation. It is not an application.

## Questions homeowners ask

**Can I have two primary residences at once?**
Not in the ordinary sense. The family occupancy provisions classify a specific transaction
based on a relative's occupancy. That is different from claiming two homes as your own
residence, and it does not carry over to taxes or exemptions.

**Will the lender count my current mortgage against me?**
Yes. Your existing housing costs are part of the obligations reviewed alongside the new payment.

**Can my parents' rent payments help me qualify?**
Generally no. Rental income from a primary residence is not permitted to qualify the borrower,
subject to narrow exceptions.

**Do my parents have to be unable to work?**
Under Fannie Mae's provision, either an inability to work or insufficient income to qualify
independently satisfies the test. Freddie Mac's parent provision states no income condition.
The lender confirms which applies.

**What if no lender will do this structure?**
Then the conversation turns to other options, including your parents borrowing with support.
There is usually more than one route, and some are better than others for a given family.
