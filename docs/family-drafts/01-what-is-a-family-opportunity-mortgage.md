# What is a Family Opportunity Mortgage? Buying a home for a parent or adult child

A Family Opportunity Mortgage is not a product you can apply for by name. It is an industry
nickname for using standard conventional occupancy rules that, in specific family situations,
let a lender treat a home as your primary residence even though your relative lives there and
you live somewhere else.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. Occupancy is one of the biggest dividing lines
in mortgage lending. A property classified as an investment property is underwritten
differently from one classified as a primary residence. These rules do not create a special
program. They change which category the transaction falls into.

There is no Fannie Mae or Freddie Mac product called a Family Opportunity Mortgage, no
separate application, and no special program guidelines. Anyone describing it as a government
grant or a little-known program is describing something that does not exist.

## Why you can live somewhere else

Normally, a primary residence loan assumes a borrower will move in. Both major agencies that
set conventional lending rules publish narrow exceptions where an occupying family member
satisfies the occupancy expectation instead.

Fannie Mae sets these out in its Selling Guide topic on
[occupancy types](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.1-01/occupancy-types),
which is the primary source for the parent and adult child provisions described below.
Freddie Mac addresses the same ground in
[Guide Section 4201.11 on mortgages secured by primary residences](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/4201.11).

Both are current as published. Neither contains an age requirement, a retirement requirement,
or a distance requirement, despite how often those conditions appear in marketing copy about
this strategy.

## The two family situations

### A child buying a home for a parent

Fannie Mae's provision applies when a parent is unable to work **or** does not have sufficient
income to qualify for a mortgage on their own. Either one is enough. It is not a test the
parent has to fail twice, and a parent who works can still fall within it if their income
will not support the mortgage independently.

Freddie Mac approaches the same situation differently, and this is a real difference rather
than a wording preference. Freddie's provision for a home occupied by the borrower's parent
does not state an ability to work or income condition at all. Which agency's rules apply to
your loan depends on the lender and the specific loan being used, which is one reason a
conversation about your situation is more useful than a checklist.

### A parent or legal guardian buying a home for an adult child with a disability

Fannie Mae's provision applies where the adult child is unable to work **or** does not have
sufficient income to qualify independently. Again, either one.

Freddie Mac's corresponding provision covers a home occupied by an individual who has a
disability where the borrower is their parent or legal guardian, and likewise does not state
an income condition.

A point worth being direct about, because marketing copy frequently gets it wrong: these
provisions do not extend to a non-disabled adult child. A parent buying a house for a healthy
college student does not fall within either agency's exception. That purchase is evaluated
under ordinary second home or investment property rules, and second home classification
carries its own occupancy expectations that a student-occupied house often will not meet.

## Who borrows, who occupies, and why ownership is a separate question

In these arrangements the borrower is typically the family member who qualifies, and the
occupant is the relative. The loan obligation sits with the borrower. If the payment is not
made, it affects the borrower's credit.

Ownership is a genuinely separate matter from the loan, and it deserves its own conversation
with an attorney rather than being treated as a mortgage detail. Neither agency requires the
occupying relative to be kept off title. Fannie Mae's guidance states that only one borrower
must occupy and take title. Any claim that your relative must be excluded from ownership is a
lender's own requirement or a simplification, not an agency rule.

One consequence catches families by surprise. Because the property is treated as a primary
residence, rent paid by the occupying relative generally cannot be used as qualifying income.
Freddie Mac states plainly that rental income from a primary residence is not permitted to
qualify the borrower, subject to narrow exceptions, and Fannie Mae takes the same position in
its
[rental income guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-01/general-rental-income-information).
You cannot claim primary residence treatment and also count your relative's contribution as
income. The borrower has to qualify carrying their own housing costs and this payment.

## Agency guideline is not the same as lender availability

This is the practical gap that decides most of these transactions.

Agency guidelines describe what is eligible. Individual lenders decide what they will
actually do, and they routinely add their own conditions on top, known as overlays. A lender
may require documentation the agency guidance does not specify, may apply a stricter
standard, or may simply decline this structure.

Notably, Fannie Mae's occupancy topic sets out the eligibility test but does not prescribe how
to document that a relative is unable to work or lacks sufficient income. That silence is
exactly why documentation requests differ so much between lenders. As a brokerage, Stonehaven's
role is to find the lenders whose current requirements fit the situation in front of us.

## What the lender reviews

Standard underwriting still applies. The exception changes occupancy classification. It does
not relax the rest of the file. Expect review of:

- The borrower's income, credit, and assets.
- Existing housing obligations. If you already own a home, that payment counts alongside the
  new one, which is the subject of
  [buying for parents while keeping your own home](/blog/buy-house-for-parents-keep-own-home).
- The property itself, through an appraisal.
- Whatever documentation the specific lender requires to support the family provision.

There is no single credit score minimum, down payment, or debt ratio ceiling that applies
across this category, and anyone quoting you one without seeing your file is guessing.

## A hypothetical example

**This is an illustration only. It is not a customer, and no terms are implied.**

Imagine an adult child who owns a home and works full time. Their parent rents, receives a
modest fixed income, and cannot qualify for a mortgage on that income alone. The family finds
a smaller home near the adult child.

Under the occupancy provisions described above, the adult child may be able to finance that
home with primary residence treatment while continuing to live in their own house. The lender
would review the adult child's income and credit, count both housing payments, and require
documentation supporting the parent's situation. The parent's contribution toward the payment,
if any, would generally not count as qualifying income.

Whether it works depends on the numbers and on which lenders currently offer the structure.
That is a scenario review, not a guess.

## Common misconceptions

| What people believe | What is actually the case |
|---|---|
| It is a special government or agency program | It is a nickname for standard occupancy exceptions. No such product exists by that name. |
| The parent must be retired or a certain age | Neither agency states an age or retirement requirement. |
| The parent must be completely unable to work | Fannie Mae's test is unable to work **or** insufficient income to qualify independently. Freddie Mac's parent provision states no income condition. |
| It works for any adult child | Only for an adult child with a disability under these provisions. A non-disabled adult child, including a college student, is outside them. |
| The relative must stay off title | No agency rule requires that. Discuss ownership with an attorney. |
| The relative's rent helps you qualify | Rental income from a primary residence generally cannot be used to qualify. |
| Occupancy classification settles taxes and benefits | It does not. It is a mortgage classification only. |

## What this classification does not decide

Mortgage occupancy classification does not determine your tax deductions, a homestead
exemption, how the property is treated in an estate, or anyone's eligibility for
means-tested benefits. Those are separate questions for a tax adviser, an attorney, and where
benefits are involved, a qualified benefits adviser.

If the person who will live in the home receives Supplemental Security Income, housing
support can affect the benefit amount. The Social Security Administration explains the general
rules in its
[Spotlight on Living Arrangements](https://www.ssa.gov/ssi/spotlights/spot-living-arrangements.htm),
and only SSA can determine an individual's situation. This is covered further in
[housing for an adult child with a disability](/blog/home-for-adult-child-with-disability).

2. **A scenario review.** Whether the occupancy provision fits, and which lenders currently
   work with the structure.
3. **Documentation.** What that specific lender needs to support the file.
4. **Application**, only once you have decided the structure makes sense.

An inquiry is a conversation. It is not an approval, a prequalification, or an application.

## Questions families ask

**Is this a real loan program I can ask for by name?**
No. Ask about conventional financing using the family occupancy provisions. A lender who
knows the guidelines will know what you mean.

**Does my relative have to be unable to work?**
Under Fannie Mae's provision, either an inability to work or insufficient income to qualify
independently satisfies the test. Freddie Mac's parent provision does not state an income
condition. The lender confirms eligibility.

**Can I do this if I already own a home?**
Often yes. Owning a home does not disqualify you, though both housing payments are counted.
See [buying for parents while keeping your own home](/blog/buy-house-for-parents-keep-own-home).

**Can my parent be on the loan with me?**
Possibly, and that is a different structure with different consequences.
[Comparing the ways to help a parent](/blog/family-opportunity-mortgage-vs-cosigning) walks
through the options.

**What if my adult child does not have a disability?**
These specific provisions will not apply. Other financing may still fit, and the honest
conversation is about which.
