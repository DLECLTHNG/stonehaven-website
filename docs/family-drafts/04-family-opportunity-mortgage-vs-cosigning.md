# Family Opportunity Mortgage vs. co-signing: ways to help a parent buy a home

**Short answer:** there are several genuinely different ways to help a parent into a home, and
they are not variations on one idea. In one, you borrow and your parent lives there. In
another, your parent borrows and you support the application. In a third, your parent borrows
and you help with funds toward the purchase. Which fits depends mostly on one thing: whether
your parent can qualify for a mortgage on their own.

This article compares them. For what the family occupancy provisions actually are, start with
[the core explainer](/blog/what-is-a-family-opportunity-mortgage).

## The four structures

### 1. You borrow, under a family occupancy arrangement

You are the borrower, your parent occupies the home, and conventional occupancy guidance can
allow primary residence treatment even though you live elsewhere. Fannie Mae's version applies
where the parent is unable to work or does not have sufficient income to qualify for a mortgage
on their own; Freddie Mac's version, in
[Guide Section 4201.11](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/4201.11), states no
income condition for a home occupied by the borrower's parent.

The debt is yours. Your income, credit, and existing obligations are what get reviewed, and
your parent's rent generally cannot be used as qualifying income because the property is being
treated as a primary residence.

### 2. Your parent borrows, with a co-borrower or a co-signer

Here your parent is the borrower and someone else strengthens the application. These two roles
are not interchangeable, which is the most common misunderstanding in this whole area.

- A **co-borrower**, in the non-occupant form, is on the loan and typically on title. Their
  income and their debts are both considered.
- A **co-signer or guarantor** is obligated on the debt but typically not on title.

In both cases the person is liable for the mortgage. That liability is real: it appears on
their credit, it counts against them when they apply for their own financing, and it does not
end because a family arrangement changed. Fannie Mae addresses these roles in its guidance on
[guarantors, co-signers, and non-occupant borrowers](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-2-04/guarantors-co-signers-or-non-occupant-borrowers-subject-transaction),
and Freddie Mac addresses non-occupying borrowers in
[Section 5103.1](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/5103.1). The specifics of how
each is treated, and what a particular loan allows, vary by agency, by program, and by lender.

Worth noting: if your parent can qualify and will occupy the home, this is simply an
owner-occupied loan with an occupant borrower. The family occupancy provisions are not needed.
Those provisions exist for the case where **no** borrower will occupy.

### 3. Your parent borrows, with a gift toward the purchase

Your parent is the borrower and qualifies on their own, and you contribute funds toward the
purchase. This keeps the debt entirely with your parent, keeps your own borrowing capacity
free, and keeps ownership simple.

Gift funds have rules: documentation of the source, evidence the funds transferred, and a
signed letter confirming there is no repayment expectation. What is permitted depends on the
transaction and the program, so ask specifically rather than assuming.

The limit is obvious. This only works if your parent can carry the mortgage.

### 4. Other financing, where the family occupancy provisions do not apply

If your parent can qualify and the occupancy provisions are not needed, ordinary financing
applies. If the provisions do not fit for another reason, the alternatives are investment
property financing or, in some situations, a second home loan.

**A word about "second home."** Second home is a mortgage occupancy classification with its own
requirements, not a label for any additional property someone buys. Fannie Mae's guidance
expects a second home to be occupied by the borrower for some portion of the year and requires
the borrower to have exclusive control over the property. A house a parent lives in full time
is generally not that. Be wary of anyone reaching for a vacation home loan to solve a parent's
full-time housing, and be equally wary of describing occupancy inaccurately to fit a category.
Occupancy misrepresentation on a mortgage application is fraud.

## The comparison

| | You borrow, family occupancy | Parent borrows with co-borrower or co-signer | Parent borrows with a gift | Investment or second home financing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Who applies and owes the debt** | You | Your parent, plus the co-borrower or co-signer, all liable | Your parent alone | Whoever borrows |
| **Who occupies** | Your parent | Your parent | Your parent | Depends on the classification, and the classification has requirements |
| **How ownership is determined** | Separate legal question. No agency rule requires the parent off title; lenders may differ | Co-borrower typically on title, co-signer typically not; varies by program | Your parent | Follows the borrower, generally |
| **Whose income and obligations are reviewed** | Yours, including your existing housing costs | Your parent's, plus the co-borrower's or co-signer's | Your parent's | The borrower's |
| **Can the parent's payments count as qualifying income** | Generally no, because it is a primary residence | Not applicable in the usual case | Not applicable | Different rules apply for investment property |
| **The question that decides if it fits** | Can you carry both housing payments? | Can your parent qualify with support, and is the co-signer willing to take on the liability? | Can your parent qualify on their own? | Is the occupancy you are describing actually true? |

Every row in that table carries lender variation. Programs differ on co-signer treatment, on
gift rules, and on whether they offer the family occupancy structure at all. Treat the table as
a map of the questions, not as a rulebook.

## Two short hypothetical situations

**These are illustrations, not customers, and no terms are implied.**

**Situation one.** A parent receives a modest fixed income and has good credit. Their income
alone will not support a mortgage in the area where the family wants them to live. Their adult
child owns a home, works, and has room in their budget. Here the family occupancy route is
worth pricing first, because the child can be the borrower and the parent's insufficient income
is precisely what the provision addresses. The deciding question is whether the child can carry
both housing payments.

**Situation two.** A parent has steady retirement income that nearly supports the purchase, and
savings that fall short of what they want to put down. Here a gift toward the purchase may be
the simplest structure, because the parent qualifies, holds title, and owns the home outright in
the family's eyes and in law. Bringing the adult child onto the loan would add liability the
family does not need.

Change one fact in either and the answer moves. That is the point. There is no structure that is
best for every family, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling rather than advising.

## What to settle before choosing

- **Can your parent qualify alone?** This single question eliminates or selects most of the options.
- **What is the co-signer's own plan?** Taking on a mortgage obligation affects their borrowing
  capacity for years. If they intend to buy or refinance soon, say so now.
- **Who should own the home in ten years?** A legal and planning question, not a mortgage one.
  Get an attorney involved before the structure is set rather than after.
- **What happens if circumstances change?** Illness, a move, a sale, or a death. Structures that
  work today differ in how gracefully they handle change.
- **Is the occupancy description accurate?** If a structure only works by describing occupancy
  inaccurately, it does not work.

## Questions families ask

**Is co-signing the same as being a co-borrower?**
No. Both create liability for the debt, but a co-borrower is typically on title while a
co-signer typically is not, and programs treat the two differently. Ask which role a specific
loan contemplates.

**Does co-signing hurt my ability to buy my own home?**
The obligation generally counts against you when you apply for your own financing. How it is
treated depends on the program and the circumstances, so raise it early if you have plans of
your own.

**Can I just buy it as a second home?**
Second home is a classification with occupancy requirements, including that the borrower occupy
it for some portion of the year and have exclusive control over it. A parent's full-time
residence generally does not meet that.

**Can I use my parent's rent to help me qualify?**
Generally no under the family occupancy route, because the property is treated as a primary
residence and rental income from a primary residence is not permitted to qualify the borrower.

**Which option is cheapest?**
That depends on the borrower, the property, and current program availability, and it cannot be
answered honestly in the abstract. It is a scenario review, not a rule.
