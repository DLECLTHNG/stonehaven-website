# Buying a home for an adult child with a disability: mortgage options and planning questions

**Short answer:** conventional occupancy guidance includes a provision under which a parent or
legal guardian can finance a home for an adult child with a disability and have it treated as a
primary residence for mortgage purposes, even though the parent lives somewhere else. The
mortgage is one piece of a larger plan, and the pieces that sit around it, ownership, benefits,
and support, need their own advisers.

This article covers the financing side accurately and points to where the other questions
belong. It assumes the housing decision itself, where someone lives and with what support, is
the family's to make with the people who know the situation.

## The provision, in the agencies' own terms

Fannie Mae's guidance addresses a parent or legal guardian wanting to provide housing for an
adult child with a disability. The condition is that the child is **unable to work or does not
have sufficient income to qualify for a mortgage on their own**. Either one satisfies it. This
is not a requirement to prove both, and a person who works and earns income can still fall
within the provision if that income would not support a mortgage independently. The text is in
the Selling Guide topic on
[occupancy types](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b2-1.1-01/occupancy-types).

Freddie Mac addresses the same ground differently. Its provision in
[Guide Section 4201.11](https://guide.freddiemac.com/app/guide/section/4201.11) treats a
mortgage as secured by a primary residence where the home is occupied by an individual who has
a disability and the borrower is their parent or legal guardian. Freddie's version states no
income or ability-to-work condition and does not use the word "adult."

Which set applies depends on the loan a particular lender is using. That is worth asking about
directly rather than assuming.

Two things the guidance does **not** do. It does not list qualifying conditions or diagnoses.
And it does not make disability alone a basis for approval. Eligibility is confirmed by the
lender, and the rest of the loan is underwritten normally.

## Does the parent have to live in the home?

No. That is the entire point of the provision. The adult child's occupancy is what supports
treating the property as a primary residence, so the parent or guardian can continue living in
their own home.

There is no distance requirement in either agency's occupancy guidance, and no age or
retirement condition. Phrases suggesting the home must be within a certain distance circulate
widely in marketing material about this strategy and are not found in the guidance.

## How documentation requirements get set

This is where families encounter the most variation, and there is a specific reason for it.

The agency guidance states the eligibility test but does not prescribe how to document it. It
does not say what evidence establishes that someone is unable to work, or that their income is
insufficient to qualify. That silence is filled by individual lenders, which is why one lender
asks for very little and another asks for a great deal, and why a lender may decline the
structure entirely.

As a brokerage, Stonehaven's job here is to identify lenders whose current requirements fit
your situation, and to tell you what a given lender will ask for before you start.

One boundary worth stating plainly: you should not send medical records, diagnoses, benefits
letters, or Social Security numbers through a website inquiry form. A first conversation does
not need them. If a lender ultimately requires documentation, it will come through a secure
application process, not a marketing page.

## Affordability is more than the mortgage payment

The loan is underwritten on the borrower's ability to carry it, which means the parent's income,
credit, existing housing costs, and other debts. Standard underwriting applies.

But the household's real cost of the home runs past the mortgage payment: property taxes and
insurance, utilities, routine maintenance, repairs that arrive without warning, any association
dues, and modifications the home may need. Families frequently plan carefully for the purchase
and less carefully for year three.

One mechanism to understand clearly. Because the property receives primary residence treatment,
rent paid by the occupying family member generally **cannot** be used as qualifying income.
Freddie Mac states that rental income from a primary residence is not permitted to qualify the
borrower, subject to narrow exceptions, and Fannie Mae takes the same position in its
[rental income guidance](https://selling-guide.fanniemae.com/sel/b3-3.8-01/general-rental-income-information).
Whatever the family decides about contributions toward costs, the borrower has to qualify
carrying the home.

## The home itself

These are housing questions rather than mortgage questions, and they tend to matter more over
time than the financing does:

- **Location.** Proximity to the people, transport, work, and services that make the
  arrangement workable day to day.
- **Accessibility.** What the home needs now, and what it might need later. Modifications are
  easier to plan before purchase than after.
- **Maintenance.** Who is responsible, how it gets arranged, and what happens when the parent
  is no longer the one arranging it.
- **Support.** Any services or support are arranged separately from the mortgage, through their
  own channels. Owning a home does not by itself provide or guarantee anything about daily
  support, and the financing conversation cannot speak to what support is appropriate.

## Title, estate planning, and benefits need coordinated advice

These three interact, and decisions made for one reason can have consequences in another.

Nothing in either agency's occupancy guidance requires the occupying family member to be kept
off title. Fannie Mae's guidance states that only one borrower must occupy and take title to
the property. If a lender imposes that condition, it is the lender's requirement rather than an
agency rule, and it is worth knowing the difference when you are weighing options with an
attorney.

Ownership structure, what happens to the home later, and how it fits with any trust or estate
arrangement are legal questions. Mortgage occupancy classification does not determine tax
deductions, homestead exemptions, estate treatment, or benefits eligibility. It is a mortgage
classification and nothing more.

### How housing support can affect SSI

If the person who will live in the home receives Supplemental Security Income, the housing
arrangement can affect the benefit amount. This is general information about how the rules
work, not a determination. Only the Social Security Administration can decide an individual's
situation.

In outline: SSA counts certain shelter provided by others as in-kind support and maintenance,
which can reduce an SSI payment. SSA describes the general framework in its
[Spotlight on Living Arrangements](https://www.ssa.gov/ssi/spotlights/spot-living-arrangements.htm).
Two rules do most of the work. One reduces the benefit by a set fraction where a person lives
in another person's household and receives both food and shelter there. The other places a
regulatory ceiling on how much support can be counted, and that ceiling can be rebutted with
evidence of actual value.

Several changes took effect on **September 30, 2024** that families researching older material
will not find. SSA stopped counting food in these calculations, so only shelter is considered.
And SSA extended nationwide a rental subsidy rule that had previously applied in only a handful
of states, under which a business arrangement exists when the required monthly rent equals or
exceeds a specified threshold; where rent is below it, the difference may be counted.

Two details make this directly relevant to the arrangement in this article. SSA's procedures
specifically address situations where the landlord is the tenant's parent, which is exactly the
fact pattern here. And the reduction for living in another person's household does not apply to
someone who has an ownership interest in the home, who has rental liability for it, or who pays
a pro rata share of household operating expenses.

None of that is a plan. It is the reason to involve a benefits adviser **before** deciding how
the arrangement will be structured, rather than after. Do not treat a mortgage decision as a
benefits decision, and do not assume any particular structure will preserve a benefit.

## Who can help answer which question?

| Question | Who to ask |
|---|---|
| Which financing structures exist, what a lender reviews, what a given lender requires | Mortgage broker or lender |
| Whether a proposed arrangement affects SSI or other means-tested benefits | A qualified benefits adviser, and ultimately the Social Security Administration |
| Who should hold title, what happens to the home later, trusts and estate planning | An attorney |
| Tax treatment of the property, deductions, exemptions | A tax adviser |
| What support or services fit the person's needs | The person themselves, their family, and the providers and agencies they work with |

The financing conversation is genuinely useful and genuinely limited. It answers the first row
of that table and none of the others.

## Questions families ask

**Does my adult child need to qualify for the mortgage?**
Under this approach the parent or legal guardian is generally the qualifying borrower, subject
to the lender's review. The provision exists so that the occupant does not have to qualify.

**Does my adult child have to be unable to work?**
Under Fannie Mae's provision, either an inability to work or insufficient income to qualify
independently satisfies the test. Freddie Mac's provision states no income condition. The
lender confirms eligibility.

**Do I have to live in the home too?**
No. The occupancy provision exists precisely so the borrower can live elsewhere.

**Can my adult child be on the title?**
No agency occupancy rule requires them to be excluded from title. Whether it is a good idea is
a legal and planning question for an attorney, not a mortgage question.

**What if my child does not have a disability?**
These specific provisions would not apply. Other financing may fit, and the right conversation
is about which.

**What should I have ready for a first call?**
A general sense of your income, your existing housing costs and other obligations, the area and
price range you are considering, and who the family expects to be on title. Nothing medical.
