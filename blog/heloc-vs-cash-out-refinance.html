A HELOC generally adds a separate credit line while keeping your existing mortgage. A cash-out refinance replaces that mortgage with a larger loan and pays out the remaining proceeds after payoffs and costs. The useful comparison is the combined payment, total borrowing cost and cash actually available for your project, not just which advertised rate is lower.

The CFPB comparison of HELOC alternatives describes these structures and cautions that either may cost more depending on its terms. Stonehaven acts as your mortgage broker. We help review the figures and financing options; a public inquiry is not an approval or commitment.

A fixed home equity loan is another comparison when you want a separate lump sum. The HELOC versus home equity loan guide focuses on that choice. Identify the structure first so that a quote for one product is not mistaken for another.

Start with the mortgage you already have

Record the outstanding balance, interest rate, remaining term, principal-and-interest payment and any other amounts included in the monthly payment. Use a recent statement for planning and an actual payoff quote for a transaction. Those figures are not always identical because a payoff may include accrued interest or other items.

If your current mortgage has a favorable rate, replacing its entire balance deserves a separate cost calculation. Keeping it does not automatically make a HELOC better, because the added line has its own cost and repayment risk. Ask for both structures using the same amount of usable cash and the same expected holding period.

Use the cash-out refinance review when replacing the first mortgage is part of your goal. If keeping that mortgage is central to your plan, the HELOC page explains the initial review. Stonehaven currently accepts HELOC requests of at least $50,000 from applicants reporting a credit score of 640 or higher. Meeting those intake minimums does not establish eligibility or terms.

How to calculate net cash from a refinance

Estimated net proceeds = new loan − mortgage payoffs − other amounts paid from the loan at closing. List the actual uses instead of treating the new loan minus the statement balance as guaranteed cash. Include financed costs only once. If you pay an item separately, show that cash expense separately.

In an original hypothetical example, a $375,000 replacement loan pays off $300,000 of existing debt and uses $9,000 for assumed closing charges and other closing items. The estimated cash available is $66,000. The loan exceeds the old debt by $75,000, but the borrower does not receive that entire difference. These amounts are illustrative, not fee estimates or lending terms.

To check that amount for your home, request a net-proceeds review with the estimated value, current payoff and cash you need.

A later refund of an old escrow account should not be treated as money available at the new closing unless the actual settlement arrangements establish that timing. Ask your servicer and closing team how the old and new escrow accounts will be handled. Compare the numbers with the CFPB Loan Estimate explainer for applicable closed-end mortgage transactions. A HELOC uses different disclosures; do not assume it comes with the same form.

Compare monthly payments without hiding a longer repayment period

A lower required payment can result from extending repayment, rather than reducing cost. Put the remaining term of the existing mortgage beside the proposed new term. Also distinguish principal-and-interest from the total housing payment, which can include taxes, insurance and other charges.

For example, a hypothetical $300,000 balance amortized over 20 years at 3% has a principal-and-interest payment of about $1,663.79. A hypothetical $375,000 replacement loan amortized over 30 years at 7% has a payment of about $2,494.88. These are fixed-rate mathematical illustrations, not current rates, offers or a recommendation. They exclude taxes, insurance and fees and do not compare equal cash proceeds against a HELOC.

To finish a real comparison, add the proposed HELOC payment to the existing mortgage and use the same project funding amount on both sides. Then compare interest, fees and balances remaining at the date you expect to sell or repay. A payment-only comparison leaves the remaining debt out of the decision. Our HELOC payment worksheet lets you test assumptions before discussing a quote.

Match the funding schedule to the actual expense

A staged renovation needs a dated contractor-payment plan. A single known expense needs a defined amount and payment date. Ask whether the proposed line requires an initial draw, imposes minimum draws or charges fees for opening or closing the account. Do not request a larger line simply because a calculator shows theoretical equity.

If your project needs $60,000 in three stages, list each stage and date. Show what you can cover if the borrowing is delayed. That schedule gives the broker a more useful question than a maximum-loan request: which structure can fund these obligations at an acceptable cost and payment? The initial-draw guide explains what to verify before assuming unused borrowing will stay untouched.

Stress-test a HELOC before comparing it with a new mortgage

Test a higher assumed rate, a larger drawn balance and the repayment phase separately. The line's low initial required payment may not show what you must pay later. The CFPB HELOC guide explains variable rates, possible restrictions on additional borrowing and the transition out of the draw period.

Read what happens to HELOC payments after the draw period before relying on an interest-only estimate. Both HELOCs and cash-out mortgages secure borrowing against the property. If the aim is to repay other debts, compare alternatives that do not put the home behind those obligations.

Compare costs over the period you expect to keep the loan

List origination charges, points, appraisal and title costs, ongoing fees and any applicable early-closure or prepayment provisions. Separate charges paid in cash from those added to the loan. Financed costs still have an economic cost even though you do not pay them from your bank account that day.

Choose a realistic comparison date, such as your planned sale or payoff. Ask for the payments made, interest and fees paid, and debt remaining at that date. Use the same project amount and include the retained first mortgage when comparing a HELOC. The HELOC offer comparison guide helps organize the agreement details.

Questions homeowners ask about HELOCs and cash-out refinancing

Is a HELOC always better if my first mortgage rate is low?

No. Keeping a low-rate first mortgage is one consideration. The line's rate, fees, required draws, repayment structure and your ability to handle both payments still need review.

Is my home equity the same as the cash I can borrow?

No. Home value minus mortgage debt is an equity estimate. The allowable loan depends on the program's leverage limits and underwriting, and net cash can be reduced by payoffs and costs. Use the home-equity borrowing guide to distinguish those figures.

Can I compare a HELOC interest-only payment with a refinance payment?

You can list them, but they do different things. An interest-only payment does not reduce principal. An amortizing payment includes principal repayment. Compare the later repayment obligation and remaining balances as well.

Can an investment property use the same financing as my home?

Do not assume that. Tell the broker the actual occupancy and intended use. A rental-property inquiry may need an investment HELOC or DSCR cash-out refinance review. Owner-occupied assumptions do not establish investment-property eligibility.

Bring three numbers and a clear goal

Start with your estimated property value, current mortgage balance and requested amount. Add your state, occupancy, current rate, remaining term and what the funds will accomplish. Use the HELOC review form or the cash-out refinance inquiry for the route that matches your goal. We follow up by text where requested.
