Up to 100% LTC construction financing means the loan may cover all costs accepted in a particular program's cost basis. It does not automatically mean zero cash from the builder. The completed-value limit, land treatment, fees, reserves and draw schedule can each change what you need to contribute. Before comparing percentages, ask for a sources-and-uses schedule and a month-by-month cash plan.

What does 100% LTC mean in a construction loan?

Loan-to-cost, or LTC, is the loan amount divided by the eligible project cost. A $2 million commitment against a $2 million accepted cost basis is 100% LTC. A $1.8 million commitment against the same basis is 90% LTC. Neither ratio tells you when the money becomes available or how much of the commitment is reserved for interest and fees.

The denominator matters as much as the percentage. One proposal may include acquisition, construction and selected soft costs. Another may describe financing for the construction budget only, with land equity supplied separately. Ask for the actual dollar cost basis behind the quoted LTC. Do not compare two percentages until both proposals use the same costs.

The OCC's Commercial Real Estate Lending handbook discusses feasibility, borrower capacity, valuation and construction administration. These are useful underwriting concepts, not an endorsement of a 100% LTC product or a universal rule for private construction financing.

LTC, completed value and loan proceeds are different figures

A construction proposal may be constrained by both eligible cost and completed value. Calculate each limit separately, then identify which one controls. A favorable cost ratio cannot overcome a lower valuation limit unless the actual program allows a different structure.

  • LTC: commitment divided by accepted project costs.
  • Completed-value ratio: commitment divided by the accepted value of the finished project. The proposal may call this completed LTV, LTARV or LTCV; ask what that label means.
  • Initial advance: the amount released at closing, before later construction draws.
  • Net construction proceeds: the portion available for the work after other financed uses and holdbacks.

A $2 million facility can therefore have a much smaller closing advance. If the facility also pays acquisition debt or funds an interest reserve, those uses do not create additional dollars for the contractor. Our construction loan calculator helps separate cost, value, land and cash assumptions. Its results are estimates, not underwriting decisions.

Worked example: the value cap creates a $320,000 gap

Assume eligible project costs of $2 million and a completed valuation of $2.4 million. For illustration only, assume a 100% LTC ceiling and a 70% completed-value ceiling. These percentages are deliberately hypothetical, not current Stonehaven terms.

Illustrative commitment calculation
TestCalculationResult
Cost ceiling$2,000,000 × 100%$2,000,000
Completed-value ceiling$2,400,000 × 70%$1,680,000
Lower limitLower of the two results$1,680,000
Eligible-cost gap$2,000,000 − $1,680,000$320,000

The project needs $320,000 beyond the modeled commitment to cover that accepted cost basis. That is not yet a complete cash-to-close answer. Some qualifying contribution may already exist as recognized land equity or paid costs. Other costs may be excluded from the eligible budget. The timing of each contribution also matters.

Now reduce the assumed completed value to $2.2 million while keeping everything else the same. The 70% value ceiling becomes $1.54 million and the eligible-cost gap rises to $460,000. This $140,000 change shows why a builder's projected resale price should not be treated as a confirmed appraisal. Model a lower-value case before committing to the purchase.

Can land equity replace the down payment?

Land you already own can affect the equity calculation, but its treatment must be confirmed. Present the acquisition date, purchase price, current debt, proposed value and documentary support for improvements. Ask whether the program uses cost, current appraised value or another basis, and whether ownership duration changes that treatment.

Land equity is not cash in your checking account. Even if it satisfies part of an equity requirement, it cannot automatically pay a subcontractor while a draw is pending. A payoff also consumes funding: a property valued at $500,000 with $200,000 debt has a different financing starting point from the same property owned free and clear.

Our guide to construction financing on property already owned explains the payoff and equity questions. Build a cash plan for three stages

Before closing: costs that arrive before loan proceeds

List earnest money, design deposits, surveys, engineering, permits and other due-diligence spending. Record what has already been paid, what can be refunded and what remains due. Do not assume every pre-closing expense will be reimbursed or counted as equity. Ask for the treatment in the financing proposal and keep invoices and payment records.

If the project is still being entitled, separate a possible construction closing from the immediate land or predevelopment funding need. A hoped-for construction commitment does not pay an obligation due next week. The land and development financing guide covers the earlier project stage.

At closing: reconcile the actual funding statement

Put acquisition or payoff, financing costs and reserves on the uses side. Put the initial loan advance, acceptable deposits and borrower contribution on the sources side. Each source must be available on the date it is needed. A later construction draw belongs later in the schedule, even if it is part of the total commitment.

Mark each cost as financed, paid before closing, paid at closing or excluded. This prevents a common planning error: financing a cost in one subtotal while also subtracting it from the builder's cash requirement elsewhere. Ask who confirms the final settlement figures and when you will receive them.

During construction: bridge the gap between bills and draws

Build a weekly schedule for major contractor payments, inspection requests and expected funding. Ask about draw frequency, submission cutoffs, evidence of completed work, lien documentation, retainage and treatment of stored materials. Do not assume all invoices become eligible for immediate reimbursement.

The NCUA construction and development examination guide explains why controlled disbursements and project-completion analysis matter. Its supervisory framework applies to credit unions; a private financing agreement can use different conditions. Our construction draw cash-flow guide turns the timing question into a practical planning exercise.

Interest reserve, contingency and working cash are not interchangeable

An interest reserve budgets financing expense. A construction contingency budgets unplanned project costs. Working cash covers bills while you wait for the next approved release. Treating all three as one available pool can make a project appear better funded than it is.

For a simple hypothetical draw example, assume $80,000 of eligible work is payable before a $72,000 release, with $8,000 retained under the assumed agreement. You need to cover the $80,000 payment initially and recover only $72,000 at that release. The remaining $8,000 is still tied up. This is a timing illustration, not a standard retainage percentage or reimbursement schedule.

Ask what happens when a change order increases the remaining cost to complete. Identify who supplies the extra funds, whether approval is required before work begins and whether additional borrowing is available. The interest reserve versus contingency guide explains the separate budgets without treating either as unlimited protection.

Compare term sheets using the same project assumptions

Use one dated budget, one value assumption and one timeline for every comparison. A higher commitment can be less helpful if it delivers less at closing, carries expensive minimum interest or requires more cash between draws. A lower headline rate can also be offset by fees or an unsuitable extension structure.

  • Amount and uses: commitment, initial advance, payoff, reserves and actual construction dollars.
  • Pricing: rate basis, points, draw fees, unused-fund charges where applicable and minimum-interest provisions.
  • Execution: permit conditions, inspections, funding sequence and document requirements.
  • Completion: maturity, extension conditions, sale or refinance assumptions and release requirements.
  • Obligations: guarantees, recourse, reporting and approval rights under the proposed documents.

Read the construction term-sheet comparison guide before selecting a proposal. Do not assume an extension will be available simply because the project remains profitable on paper. Put a delayed-completion case beside the original timeline and identify the added carrying costs and funding source.

Plan the exit before deciding how much to borrow

For a spec home, model net sale proceeds after assumed selling costs and loan payoff, not just the expected sale price. For several homes, map individual closings, debt releases and cash available for the next start. The multiple spec-home financing guide helps organize overlapping projects.

For a build-to-rent property, test the proposed permanent loan independently from the construction facility. A completed building does not automatically qualify for the refinance amount required to repay construction debt. Rent, taxes, insurance, value, occupancy and program rules can change the takeout. Review the construction-to-DSCR exit guide while the budget is still flexible.

What should a builder send for an initial review?

Start with a short project summary: location, property type, purchase or ownership position, existing payoff, construction budget, expected completed value, requested financing and desired closing date. Add whether plans and permits are complete, your relevant completed-project experience and the intended sale or rental exit. If a figure is an estimate, label it as one.

A useful next step is a single sources-and-uses page plus a schedule. Separate paid costs from costs still to be funded, and show other active projects that may use the same cash. This helps Stonehaven identify unanswered structuring questions before a detailed application. It does not replace underwriting, an appraisal or review of legal documents.

Questions about 100% LTC construction loans

Does 100% LTC mean no money down?

Not necessarily. It describes a ratio against an accepted cost basis. A value limit, excluded costs, reserves, land treatment or draw timing can still create a borrower contribution or working-capital need.

Can I receive the whole commitment at closing?

Do not assume so. Separate the closing advance from amounts released later for eligible construction work. Confirm the disbursement schedule in the actual agreement.

Does a higher ARV guarantee a larger loan?

No. An accepted valuation is only one input. Project cost, program limits, borrower qualifications, liquidity, experience and the exit can also constrain the structure. A projected resale value is not an appraisal.

Yes, we can review an owned-land construction scenario as your mortgage broker. Include the debt balance, ownership history and budget. Availability and final terms depend on the project and underwriting; submitting a scenario is not approval.
