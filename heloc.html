The Short Answer

A HELOC (home equity line of credit) is a revolving credit line secured by your home's equity - a second lien that leaves your existing mortgage exactly as it is. You draw what you need during a draw period (interest-only payments are common), then repay the balance over a repayment period. Rates are typically variable. The Mechanics

Two phases, one honest picture.

A HELOC lives in two acts. During the draw period - commonly around ten years - the line works like a credit card secured by your house: draw, repay, redraw, with interest-only payments typically allowed on what's outstanding. Then the repayment period begins: no more draws, and the balance amortizes - principal plus interest - over the remaining term.

That transition is where households get hurt: an interest-only payment that felt easy becomes a fully amortizing one, sometimes years after anyone read the paperwork. The calculator above shows both phases side by side for exactly that reason - if the repayment-phase payment doesn't fit your budget, the answer is a smaller draw, not surprise in year eleven.

The Comparison

HELOC vs cash-out vs home equity loan.

HELOC - revolving, draw as needed, typically variable rate, keeps your first mortgage untouched. Wins for staged expenses (renovations in phases, tuition by semester) and for standby flexibility you may never fully use.

Cash-out refinance - replaces your entire first mortgage with a bigger one. If your existing rate is lower than today's market, cash-out reprices everything you owe to reach the new money - often a brutal trade. When the old rate is the treasure, the second-lien route protects it. See the refinance page for when cash-out does win.

Home equity loan - a fixed lump sum, fixed rate, fixed payment, also behind your first mortgage. Wins when you need one known amount and want payment certainty over flexibility.

Which fits is a file-level answer - and since we arrange all three paths, the recommendation is a comparison, not a pitch.

Plainly

The risks, before you sign.

Your home secures the line - that's why the pricing beats credit cards, and it's also the entire risk: sustained non-payment can ultimately threaten the home. Rates are typically variable, so payments can rise. The end-of-draw step-up is real and arrives on schedule. And easy access to equity is a feature only when the use is deliberate - consolidating card debt onto your house while the cards stay open is how equity quietly evaporates. Questions

Before you draw

Does a HELOC change my existing mortgage?

No - it's a separate second lien behind your current mortgage, which keeps its rate, term, and payment untouched. That's the core reason HELOCs surged when first-mortgage rates rose: homeowners access equity without repricing the loan they're glad they have.

Do I pay interest on the whole line?

Only on what you've actually drawn. A $150,000 line with $20,000 drawn accrues interest on $20,000 - the rest is standby capacity. Some lenders have minimum draws or inactivity fees, which is exactly the fine print a specialist reads with you before you sign.

How much line can I get?

Lenders cap your combined loan-to-value - all home-secured debt against the home's value - commonly somewhere in the 80–90% range, alongside credit and income review. The calculator above shows the arithmetic for any cap you enter; underwriting sets the real one.

HELOC or cash-out refinance?

Usually decided by your current first-mortgage rate. If it's below today's market, a cash-out refinance repricing your whole balance is expensive money; the HELOC protects the old rate and prices only the new draw. If your existing rate is high anyway, cash-out can win. Five minutes with both numbers settles it.
